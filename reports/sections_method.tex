\section{Introduction}

Authorship attribution asks: given an unseen passage, which author from a
closed candidate set most likely wrote it, judged by \emph{how} something is
written rather than \emph{what} it says.  Bangla literature spans stylistically
distinct classical and modern voices, yet computational stylometry for Bangla
remains comparatively underexplored, and per-author corpora are small.

This project builds a multi-author Bangla stylometric pipeline and uses it to
answer three questions the proposal set out:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Paradigm.} Does a \emph{generative} attributor --- one language
        model per author, attribute to the lowest perplexity --- beat or lose to
        a \emph{discriminative} one trained directly to separate the authors?
  \item \textbf{Representation.} In a small-corpus regime, do \emph{pretrained}
        embeddings or embeddings \emph{trained from scratch} on the author
        corpus better isolate stylistic signal from topical content?
  \item \textbf{Interpretability.} Which feature families actually drive the
        decisions, and can a single prediction be audited rather than taken on
        faith?
\end{enumerate}

A fourth question emerged during the work and turned out to matter more than
any of them, so it is stated up front rather than buried in the results.

\begin{keybox}[title=The result that governs every other number in this report]
Splitting the corpus by \emph{passage} gives \randomSplitAcc{} accuracy.
Splitting the same corpus by \emph{work} --- so that no book has passages on
both sides --- gives \workSplitAcc{}.  The same model, the same features, the
same text: \textbf{\leakGap{} of apparent accuracy was topic leakage}, a
classifier recognising a novel's proper nouns and being scored as though it had
recognised a style.

Every number reported here is from the work-disjoint split.  Comparisons with
published Bangla attribution accuracies should be read with this in mind.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{What Bangla brings to the problem}

Two properties of the language shape the whole design.

\textbf{Diglossia.}  Bangla prose of this period is written in one of two
registers.  \bn{সাধু} (\emph{sadhu}, literary) uses long verb and pronoun forms
--- \bn{হইল}, \bn{করিয়া}, \bn{তাহার}.  \bn{চলিত} (\emph{chalit}, colloquial)
uses their contracted counterparts --- \bn{হল}, \bn{করে}, \bn{তার}.  The choice
is authorial and largely stable within a writer, so a single scalar --- the
sadhu-to-chalit marker ratio --- carries real attribution signal and is
directly interpretable.  It is used as an explicit feature (§\ref{sec:features})
and it is the first column to read in the style-fingerprint figure.

\textbf{Suffixal morphology.}  Case and tense are marked by suffixes
(\bn{-এর}, \bn{-কে}, \bn{-িয়াছিলেন}).  A character $n$-gram model therefore
sees inside the word and can pick up the register difference between
\bn{করিয়াছিলেন} and \bn{করেছিলেন} without either form having been observed
whole --- which is why the generative side of this study uses character models
as its primary configuration.

\section{Corpus construction}
\label{sec:corpus}

The proposal commits to documenting corpus construction \emph{as part of the
methodology, not as a black box}.  This section is that documentation.

\subsection{Source and licensing}

Text comes from Bengali Wikisource (\url{https://bn.wikisource.org}), which
hosts community-transcribed public-domain works.  Every author in the study
died more than sixty years before this project, so the texts are out of
copyright; Wikisource's transcriptions are CC BY-SA 4.0.

\subsection{Getting usable text out of Wikisource}

Three facts about the site determined the crawler's design, and each one cost a
rewrite to discover.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Chapter pages contain no text.}  A main-namespace page is a
        transclusion directive, \verb|<pages index="Book.pdf" from=149 to=156/>|.
        The prose lives in the \bn{পাতা:} (\texttt{Page:}) namespace, one wiki
        page per scanned page.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{Page:} pages carry a proofreading level.}
        \verb|<pagequality level="N">| runs from 0 (no text) to 4 (validated).
        \textbf{Level 1 is raw, uncorrected OCR}, and for Bangla that OCR is
        frequently mangled into Devanagari look-alikes --- visually similar
        glyphs from a different Unicode block.  Only level $\geq 3$ is admitted.
        This is the single most important quality control in the pipeline.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{Page:} pages batch fifty at a time} through
        \texttt{prop=revisions}, roughly fifty times cheaper than rendering each
        chapter, which matters because the anonymous API rate limit returns
        HTTP 429 within a handful of requests otherwise.
\end{enumerate}

Every API response is cached on disk, so the corpus behind the reported numbers
can be rebuilt byte-for-byte and an interrupted crawl resumes for free.

\subsection{Cleaning}

After extraction, each line is kept only if it is at least 30 characters long
and at least 88\,\% of its non-space characters lie in the Bangla Unicode block
(U+0980--U+09FF).  That single rule removes four different problems at once:
running headers, page numbers, English epigraphs, and the residue of bad OCR
(whose Devanagari look-alikes are, by definition, outside the Bangla block).

It also has a side effect worth naming, because it changed the shape of the
study: verse is dropped.  Poetry lines are short, so a book of poems survives
the filter with almost no text.  The resulting corpus is therefore
\textbf{prose only}, which is the correct control anyway --- comparing a poet's
verse against a novelist's prose would measure genre, not authorship.

\subsection{Passages}

Cleaned text is cut into \textbf{passages} of ${\sim}\meanPassageTokens{}$
tokens on sentence boundaries: long enough for sentence-length statistics to
mean anything, short enough to yield several hundred independent samples per
author.  Near-duplicates are removed with an 8-gram shingle-overlap test, which
matters because Wikisource hosts multiple editions of the same novel.

Every passage records the work it came from.  That label is what makes
§\ref{sec:protocol} possible.

\subsection{Who is in, and who is not}

An author is admitted only with enough passages \emph{and} at least three
distinct works.  The second criterion is the binding one: a one-book author
cannot take part in a work-disjoint split at all, since every passage of theirs
would land in the same split.

\section{Feature engineering}
\label{sec:features}

Four families, each a scikit-learn transformer so that the ablations in
§\ref{sec:results} are a matter of swapping blocks rather than rewriting code.

\subsection{Function words}

Relative frequency, per thousand tokens, of every item in a curated
closed-class lexicon: pronouns, postpositions, conjunctions, particles,
determiners, adverbs and quantifiers, listed in \emph{both} registers.

Using a fixed lexicon rather than the corpus's own top-$N$ word list is
deliberate.  A corpus-derived list would quietly admit topic words --- a
character's name is frequent in the novel they appear in --- and would inflate
accuracy for exactly the reason §\ref{sec:protocol} exists to prevent.

\subsection{Structural statistics}

Twenty-nine scalars: the sentence-length distribution (mean, standard
deviation, skew, median, IQR, range), word length, lexical richness
(type--token ratio, hapax and \emph{dis legomena} rates, Yule's $K$),
punctuation rates broken out by mark, conjunct-consonant density --- and the
sadhu/chalit register ratio described in §1.1, which is the most interpretable
single number in the feature set.

\subsection{Character $n$-grams}

TF--IDF over character 2--4-grams with sub-linear term frequency.  Cheap,
language-independent, and the strongest single family in most attribution
literature.  For a suffixally inflected language they are especially apt, for
the reason given in §1.1.

\subsection{POS $n$-grams, and the tagger they required}
\label{sec:tagger}

The proposal calls for POS $n$-grams ``from a Bangla tagger''.  There is no
usable off-the-shelf option:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \texttt{stanza} ships only character language models for Bengali, no
        POS model;
  \item \texttt{bnlp-toolkit} depends on \texttt{gensim}, which has no
        Python 3.14 wheel and needs a C toolchain this machine lacks;
  \item the only Universal Dependencies Bengali treebank,
        \texttt{UD\_Bengali-BRU}, is \posSentences{} sentences and test-only ---
        far too small to train on.
\end{itemize}

So the tagger was written for this project: a two-stage lexicon-plus-morphology
tagger over a coarse tagset.  Closed classes (pronouns, postpositions,
conjunctions, particles, determiners, interjections) are enumerable and are
listed in both registers.  Open classes come from morphology --- Bangla's
finite-verb inflection is highly regular (\bn{-িলেন}, \bn{-েছে}, \bn{-বেন},
\bn{-িতেছিল}), and adjectives and adverbs have their own derivational suffixes
(\bn{-ীয়}, \bn{-ময়}, \bn{-ভাবে}, \bn{-রূপে}).  Nominal case clitics are stripped
before lookup.  Anything left over defaults to \texttt{NOUN}, the majority open
class.

It is validated on \texttt{UD\_Bengali-BRU} --- usable for evaluation precisely
because it is too small to train on --- at \textbf{\posAcc{} token accuracy}
over \posTokens{} tokens.  Three UD distinctions are deliberately not attempted
and are folded in the gold before scoring:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{llp{0.52\linewidth}}
\toprule
Fold & & Why \\
\midrule
\texttt{AUX} & $\rightarrow$ \texttt{VERB} & Bangla auxiliaries are ordinary
verbs in ordinary verb forms; separating them needs syntax this tagger has
none of. \\
\texttt{PROPN} & $\rightarrow$ \texttt{NOUN} & Would need a gazetteer of
19th-century Bangla literary names, which does not exist. \\
\texttt{SCONJ} & $\rightarrow$ \texttt{CCONJ} & The stylometric quantity of
interest is conjunction \emph{density}, not subtype. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each fold discards information rather than inventing it.  The accuracy figure
is reported so a reader can discount the POS features appropriately rather than
take them on trust; §\ref{sec:results} shows what those features are worth in
practice.

\section{Representations: pretrained versus from scratch}
\label{sec:embeddings}

The proposal's representation-level question is put under otherwise identical
conditions --- same split, same classifier, same evaluation.

\paragraph{From scratch.}  Skip-gram with negative sampling, written directly
in PyTorch (\texttt{gensim} being unavailable), 200 dimensions, window 5,
8 negatives, Mikolov subsampling, a $3/4$-power noise distribution and a
dynamic window radius.  It is fit on the \textbf{training split only}, so no
test text reaches the representation.

\paragraph{Pretrained.}  \texttt{csebuetnlp/banglabert}, frozen, mean-pooled
over the last hidden layer.  Pretrained on orders of magnitude more text than
this corpus --- but \emph{modern} Bangla, and pretrained for semantics.  The
corpus here is 19th- and early-20th-century literary prose in a register the
pretraining data barely contains.

\paragraph{Pooling.}  Word vectors are pooled into passage vectors two ways.
SIF weighting ($a/(a+p(w))$) damps frequent words, which helps semantic tasks.
For a \emph{stylometric} task that is a genuine trade-off, because the frequent
function words SIF damps are exactly what classical stylometry runs on.  Both
poolings are reported rather than assumed.

\paragraph{A direct diagnostic.}  Downstream accuracy conflates the
representation with the classifier, so a second measurement is taken:
silhouette score of the test passages under \emph{author} labels versus under
\emph{individual work} labels, on the same vectors.  A representation that
encodes style should separate authors at least as well as it separates books;
one that mostly encodes topic separates books far better, because each book has
its own subject matter.  The ratio is what the proposal's question actually
turns on.

\section{The two attribution paradigms}
\label{sec:paradigms}

\subsection{Generative}

One language model per author, fit only on that author's training text.  A test
passage is attributed to whichever author's model assigns it the highest
average log-probability per token --- equivalently, the lowest perplexity.  The
model never sees the other authors: it learns what this author's text looks
like, not how they differ from anyone.

Smoothing is interpolated Kneser--Ney, implemented for this project rather than
imported.  Kneser--Ney rather than add-one or Witten--Bell because of its
\emph{continuation} probability: the unigram fallback asks not ``how often did
this token occur?'' but ``after how many distinct contexts did it occur?''.
For stylometry that is exactly the useful question --- a token that is frequent
but always in one fixed phrase is a topic artefact, whereas one appearing after
many different contexts is a habit.  Writing it out also exposes the per-token
log-probability breakdown that §\ref{sec:interp} uses to make the generative
side auditable.

Two configurations: character \CharOrder-grams and word \WordOrder-grams.

\subsection{Discriminative}

All authors' features go into one classifier trained to tell them apart.

\emph{Linear SVM.}  Chosen over an RBF kernel for two reasons: it scales to the
several-thousand-dimensional sparse feature space, and its coefficients read
directly as per-feature evidence, which the interpretability requirement needs.
Run over each feature family alone, over combinations, over each embedding
regime, and over stylometric-plus-embedding concatenations.

\emph{Fine-tuned BanglaBERT.}  The heaviest model in the comparison and the one
carrying the most outside knowledge.  Fine-tuning ran on CPU --- no CUDA device
was available --- at roughly $0.42$\,s per sample, which capped the budget at
\FTEpochs{} epochs over \FTMaxLen{} sub-word tokens.  That constraint is stated
rather than hidden: the fine-tuned score below is a
\emph{compute-constrained} one, and §\ref{sec:limits} says what that does and
does not license concluding.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Work-disjoint splitting}

Each of an author's books is assigned whole to exactly one of train,
validation or test.  Because books are indivisible and differ wildly in length,
hitting 60/15/25 exactly is impossible; assignment uses the
largest-processing-time heuristic --- take an author's works biggest-first and
give each to whichever split is currently furthest below its quota.  Taking the
big books first is what makes it work: a small book arriving last can fine-tune
a bin, whereas a big one can only overshoot it.

Realised: \trainFrac{} train (\nTrain{} passages), \valFrac{} validation
(\nVal{}), \testFrac{} test (\nTest{}).  The invariant is asserted rather than
assumed --- an explicit check confirms no work and no passage appears in two
splits, and it is run as part of the notebook rather than as a one-off.

\subsection{Measuring what the discipline costs}

Rather than assert that work-disjoint splitting matters, it is measured.  The
same model was trained twice on the same passages --- once on the work-disjoint
split, once on a naive stratified random split.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
Split & Test accuracy \\
\midrule
Random, at the passage level (leaky) & \randomSplitAcc{} \\
Work-disjoint (used throughout this report) & \workSplitAcc{} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Inflation attributable to topic leakage} & \textbf{$+$\leakGapAbs{}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The leaky number is close to ceiling.  It is also close to meaningless: it
mostly measures whether the model can recognise which novel a passage came
from.

\subsection{Metrics}

Accuracy and macro-$F_1$, with 95\,\% percentile bootstrap intervals over test
passages (2\,000 resamples) so that single numbers carry error bars, plus
row-normalised confusion matrices --- because the proposal asks specifically
about confusion between stylistically \emph{similar} authors, which a scalar
cannot answer.  Chance is \chanceAcc{} for \nAuthors{} balanced classes.
